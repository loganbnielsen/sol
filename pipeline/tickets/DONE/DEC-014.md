**Depends on:** None (DEC-011 decided the provider tiers; this decides how usage is charged and bounded).

Decide how hosted usage is priced and — more urgently — **bounded**, so a customer's workload cannot generate an unbounded bill for Sol.

## Question

DEC-011 chose provider tiers, which fixes the per-customer cost **floor**. It says nothing about the **exposure**: egress, data processing, request volume and storage scale with the customer's traffic and are billed to Sol. A flat price covering unbounded usage means Sol absorbs the tail — the difference between a $200 month and a $200,000 one is a customer's runaway loop, not a pricing table. How is that bounded, and how is the variable part charged for?

## Direction from discussion (2026-09-10)

The framing that fits is **shared liability, stated up front**: the customer sets a monthly ceiling — "never bill me more than $2,000" — and Sol guarantees it by stopping the workload. Customers do not want unbounded liability either, so this is a **feature to advertise**, not merely a guardrail for Sol. The earlier "flat price with hard platform quotas" option is rejected: an invisible allotment is poor UX and is not what customers are asking for.

What has to hold for the guarantee to be real:

- **A money-denominated cap is lagging.** It is enforced *after* metering, so usage between samples overshoots it. It needs frequent sampling, deliberate headroom (stop at $1.9k against a $2k cap), and **rate-of-spend** alerting — which catches a runaway loop far sooner than an absolute threshold does.
- **Rate-based limits underneath are what make the cap honourable.** Resource limits on egress/CPU/memory are enforced instantaneously, so a fast runaway cannot blow past the cap between samples. These stay an implementation detail: the customer sees a spend cap, the platform uses limits privately to keep the promise. Quotas are therefore a mechanism, not the customer-facing model.
- **It needs per-customer usage → cost attribution**, plus a unit-cost table per provider tier (INFRA-006) to convert usage into money.

Both sub-questions this section originally raised — what happens at the cap, and whether the compliance tier can hard-stop — are resolved in the Decision below.

## Decision criteria

- **Bounding is the priority, not pricing.** A worst case that is a *design property* — rate alerts, resource limits, a ceiling that stops the workload — beats a worst case that depends on the customer behaving.
- **You cannot price what you cannot measure.** Per-tenant usage attribution is a prerequisite for the ceiling itself, and for knowing margin per customer at all.
- **The cheap tier is more predictable, not just cheaper.** Provider egress allowances (e.g. 20 TB per node) remove most of the classic runaway, so the tiers differ in risk as well as price and the model can reflect that.
- **DEC-006 covers billing.** This should land as an input to it rather than becoming a second mechanism.
- It has to survive a customer whose service is genuinely popular — a model that punishes success hard enough drives them away is worse than a slightly thinner margin.

## Decision

**The customer sets a monthly spend ceiling. At the ceiling the workload is stopped.** Alerts fire on the way up, as ordinary metric alerts on the same stack as every other application metric — not a billing-specific mechanism. A customer who wants different behaviour raises their threshold.

That is the whole model, and it deliberately has no "overage" mode: a ceiling the customer can exceed is not a ceiling.

**Enforcement, concretely.** Because the ceiling is denominated in money it *lags* the usage that causes it, so the guarantee needs three layers:

1. **Rate-of-spend alerting** (dollars per hour), which catches a runaway loop hours before an absolute threshold would.
2. **Headroom** — stop just under the ceiling, absorbing the sampling lag.
3. **Instantaneous resource limits** (egress/CPU/memory) enforced by the platform, so the guarantee cannot be outrun between samples. These stay an implementation detail: the customer sees a spend ceiling, not a quota.

**Alerts reuse the existing stack.** The platform already ships Prometheus and Alertmanager, so per-tenant spend is an exporter metric (`sol_hosted_spend_dollars_total{customer=…}`) with standard rules at 50/80/95% plus a rate-of-spend rule. OBS-043's receiver routing applies unchanged.

**An unset ceiling must be an explicit choice.** A ceiling that silently defaults to unlimited is how customers receive a bill they did not expect — and how the dispute lands on Sol afterwards. The default is a ceiling; running without one should be acknowledged, not merely unset.

**Stopping is loud and reversible.** Raising the threshold brings the workload back, and the stop must surface as a first-class event (status plus notification): a silent stop is indistinguishable from an outage and will be reported as one.

**Compliance tier.** SLA-holding customers are where stopping is worst. The answer is not "alert + overage" on their behalf — most enterprises will not accept unlimited spend either — but a **negotiated high ceiling**, with the contract as the mechanism rather than an absence of one.

**Prerequisites:** per-tenant usage → cost attribution, and per-tier unit costs (INFRA-006). Neither a figure nor an alert is possible before those exist.

**Acceptance criteria:** the model is named ✅; the worst case per customer is exactly their ceiling ✅; enforcement is identified ✅ (the three layers above, on the existing alert path); attribution is recorded as the blocking prerequisite ⏳.
